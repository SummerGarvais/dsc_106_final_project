01  /  Sea-Ice Changes

On thin ice

Since the beginning of the industrial revolution (~1850) our polar ice caps have been melting at an alarming rate. Data charting the surface-area-coverage and thickness of sea-ice from 1850-2000 shows just how dire things are.

In the arctic circle, sea-ice which would reach down towards North America and Europe has largely been restricted to the most northern tip of the globe. In the south, sea-ice now barely encompasses the borders of Antarctica. Overall, the footprint of sea-ice coverage has reduced 69% from 1850 to 2000. Even in the areas where sea ice still forms, its thickness has reduced dramatically.

02  /  Climate Change

Too hot to handle

Over the past decades, a concensus in the scientific world has taken hold; our climate is changing and humans are to blame. Green house gas emissions from the fossil fuels we use to power our modern lives are emitting gases like carbon dioxide (CO2) which, due to their molecular structure, trap heat within our atmosphere.

Since we started burning these fuels at scale around the industrial revolution, tons upon tons of these gases have filled our atmosphere and induced a "green house effect," insulating heat from the Sun. This process, alongside mass deforestation, are noted among many experts to be the leading causes of climate change, both of which are a product of human civilization.

The impacts of climate change are already apparent on our planet. The World Meteorological Organizatation estimated that the average global temperature of Earth in 2025 was 1.4°C (2.5°F) warmer than pre-industrial levels. This increased heat isn't just effecting our atmosphere, it is warming our oceans too.

04  /  Cycle Disruption

A delicate balance

While a 1.4°C change in temperature may not seem substantial to humans, even small temperature fluctuations can greatly impact Earth's natural processes, especially the formation of sea-ice. Each year, as the seasons wash over each hemisphere, the polar regions of our Earth go through phases of sea-ice freeze, and melt. However, while this cycle used to be balanced, rising temperatures have caused to go off course.

05  /  Ice Melt Flux

More melt. Less freeze.

The shift in the sea-ice creation cycle can be visualized in the shift in "ice melt flux." This statistic shows the kilograms per square meter of ice which is being lost each second. As the months go on, the poles go through these melting and freezing periods, but more recently they have become unbalanced.

Warmer global temperatures means that the melting period of the year is becoming ever longer, as the freezing window becomes more narrow. Simply put, there is more time for ice to melt than there is time for it to freeze.



Sea-level Rise

More water, more problems





Melting sea-ice is a huge issue for animals which rely on it for their habitat; but the impacts don't end there. When sea-ice melts into water, it expands the capacity of our vast oceans. The balance of the freeze/melt cycle kept our ocean volume in check, until now.

INSERT GRAPH ABOUT RISING SEA LEVELS IF POSSIBLE

Rapid sea-ice melt from our warming planet is dumping more water into our oceans and sea-levels are rising as a consequence. Higher sea-levels disrupts marine ecosystems and puts habitats and communities along the coasts of our planet at risk of devastation. Sea-level rise may seems like an issue localized to just low-lying areas, but having more surface water exposed to atmosphere can cause more widespread issues.
