01  /  Sea Ice Thickness

On thin ice

Since the beginning of the industrial revolution (~1850) our polar ice caps have been melting at an alarming rate. Data charting the surface-area-coverage and thickness of sea ice from 1850-2000 shows just how dire things are.

In the arctic circle, sea ice which would reach down towards North America and Europe has largley been restricted to the most northern part tip of the globe. In the south, sea ice now barely encompasses the borders of Antarctica. Overall, the footprint of sea ice coverage has reduced 69% from 1850 to 2000. Even in the areas where sea ice still forms, its thickness has reduced dramatically.

02  /  Climate Change

Too hot to handle

Over the past decades, a concensus in the scientific world has taken hold; our climate is warming and we are to blame. Green house gas emissions the fossil fuels we use to power our modern lives are emitting gases like carbon dioxide (CO2) which, due to their molecular structure, trap heat within our atmosphere.

Since we started burning these fuels at scale around the industrial revolution, tons upon tons of these gases have filled our atmosphere and induced a "green house effect." This process, alongside mass deforestation, are noted among many experts to be the leading causes of climate change, both of which are a product of human civilization.

03  /  The Long View

Advancing the decades reveals a slow retreat.

A closer look

More melting. Less freezing.

The World Meteorological Organizatation estimated that the average global temperature of Earth in 2025 was 1.4°C (2.5°F) warmer than pre-industrial levels. This increased heat isn't just effecting our atmosphere, it is warming our oceans too.

04  /  Ice Melt Flux

A delicate balance

While a 1.4°C change in temperature may not seem substantial to humans, even small temperature fluctuations can greatly impact Earth's natural processes, including the formation of sea ice. As the seasons pass through each hemisphere, the polar regions of our Earth go through phases of sea ice freeze, and melt. However, while this cycle used to be balanced, it has gone off course over the past 150 years.
